It is tempting to begin an SLO discussion with the metrics already available. CPU, memory, and pod health are important operational signals, but they do not directly describe whether a user can complete an action.

I prefer to start with a concrete promise: requests should succeed, queue work should finish within an expected time, or fresh data should appear before it becomes misleading.

From that promise, the service-level indicator becomes clearer. It needs a numerator, denominator, time window, and a definition of which events count.

## Leave room for change

An error budget makes the target useful for decisions. When reliability is healthy, a team can spend more of the budget on releases and experiments. When the budget is being consumed too quickly, reliability work becomes the priority.

The target should be demanding enough to protect users but not so absolute that every small failure becomes an emergency. “100%” often hides trade-offs rather than removing them.

A good SLO creates a shared language between product and engineering. It turns reliability from a vague ambition into a measurable choice.
